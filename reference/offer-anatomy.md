# offer-anatomy.md — What an Offer Actually Is

## The definition

An offer is not a product. It is the specific bundle of: **what you do, for whom, how, and at what price** — and the perception, economics, and scalability that bundle creates.

The same underlying capability can produce wildly different businesses depending on how it is packaged. The researcher's job is to find the packaging that solves all three simultaneously.

---

## The three jobs every offer must do

1. **Packaging (perception)** — How is it perceived? Does the avatar immediately recognize it as being for them?
2. **Economics (price)** — Does the packaging let you price against value rather than comparison?
3. **Scalability (fulfillment)** — Does the packaging allow repeatable, standardizable delivery?

Most offers solve one or two of these. The rare offers that solve all three at once build durable businesses.

---

## The 3S formula: the master move

**Specific problem → Specific person → Specific mechanism (the "specific way")**

This is not three separate tactics. It is one move that solves all three jobs simultaneously:

- **Specific problem** → the avatar recognizes themselves (packaging)
- **Specific person** → no price comparison exists; you're priced against value (economics)
- **Specific mechanism** → fulfillment standardizes around one type of client (scalability)

**The canonical example:** A generic weight-loss program sells for $19. The same core program aimed only at shift nurses sells for $1,997. The underlying work is nearly identical. Everything about the offer changed.

**The test:** Can the user complete this sentence?
*"I solve [specific problem] for [specific person] in [specific way that explains why others failed]."*

If they can't complete it — or if any of the three slots is generic — the offer is not yet sharp enough.

---

## The mechanism: the load-bearing element

In any market at Stage 3 or higher (which is almost every market), the mechanism is the difference between an offer that lands and one that dies on contact.

A well-constructed mechanism does two things simultaneously:

1. **Explains why past attempts failed** — specifically and credibly
2. **Explains why this is different** — via a named, specific process

Without #1, the mechanism is just a brand name. Without #2, it's just a critique. Both together is what earns belief in a skeptical market.

**What makes a mechanism credible (T1/T2 vs. T4):**
- A named, specific process the user has actually run
- Results with specificity (dollar amounts, percentages, timeframes) — T2 if general, T1 if from actual clients
- A logical explanation of *why* it works that the buyer can follow
- Contrast with why the common alternative fails

**What makes a mechanism weak:**
- It could apply to any competitor in the space
- It's a rebranded version of industry standard practice
- The user cannot explain the mechanism to a skeptical buyer in 60 seconds
- There is no evidence it has worked for anyone

## The value equation

Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice)

**Practical implications for offer design:**

To raise value, you can:
- Raise the dream outcome (more specific, more desirable endpoint)
- Raise perceived likelihood (proof, mechanism explanation, guarantee)
- Lower time delay (faster result, or milestones that arrive sooner)
- Lower effort & sacrifice (done-for-you, templates, automation)

**The researcher's diagnostic:** When someone's offer isn't converting, which lever is broken? Most founders instinctively raise the dream outcome (bigger promises) when the actual problem is low perceived likelihood (weak proof, no mechanism) or high perceived effort (complex onboarding, unclear process).

---

## The done-for-you hybrid

A powerful structure: own the single most critical piece done-for-you; coach the rest.

Why it works:
- Commands done-for-you pricing power (you own the piece the client most fears)
- Preserves done-with-you scalability (you're not doing everything)
- The client feels high accountability; the margin is protected

**Identify the critical piece:** What is the one deliverable that most determines the outcome AND that the client is most likely to botch on their own? That is the piece to own DFY.

---

## Source discipline on offer claims

- "Clients get X result" → T1 if from actual paid engagements with documentation; T3 if aggregated / cherry-picked
- "Our mechanism is unique" → T3 until a competitor audit confirms no one else names it similarly
- "The market values this at Y" → T3 until confirmed by actual sales at that price
- The user's own best/worst client split → T1 (primary data; the most reliable offer signal available)
