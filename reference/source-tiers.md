# source-tiers.md — How to Weight Offer Research Claims

## The core problem

Offer research is uniquely prone to bad data. Most of the inputs — market size, competitor revenue, "this niche is underserved," testimonial results — are unverifiable, cherry-picked, or actively misleading. The researcher's discipline is to tier every claim and refuse to propagate T3/T4 as fact.

---

## The four tiers

### T1 — Verified
**What it is:** Evidence the researcher can confirm is real, from a source with accountability.

Examples:
- The user's own sales data (invoices, CRM records, bank statements)
- The user's own best/worst client split (direct observation)
- Publicly stated competitor pricing (their website, their sales pages)
- Named case studies with specific, verifiable results
- Industry reports from named firms with methodology disclosed

**How to treat it:** State plainly. Note when it was observed (pricing changes; results date).

### T2 — Observed but decaying
**What it is:** Real signals that are true *right now* but will shift.

Examples:
- Keyword search volume and trends (Google Trends, SEMrush — platform data changes)
- Market sophistication level (inferred from competitor copy — copy changes)
- Benchmark pricing ranges (publicly observable but not locked)
- Channel conversion benchmarks (industry averages; platform-specific and version-dependent)

**How to treat it:** Date-stamp it. "As of [source/date], re-check before acting on this." Never treat it as permanent.

### T3 — Contested
**What it is:** Claims that may be true but cannot be confirmed from available evidence, or that come from a single interested source.

Examples:
- "The market is worth $X billion" (paid research; methodology opaque; often inflated)
- "This niche is underserved" (founder belief; confirmation bias risk)
- Guru revenue claims ("I made $X in Y months") — unverifiable; often aggregated or cherry-picked
- Competitor testimonial results — context-stripped; no base rate
- "Clients typically pay Y for this" — without sales data, this is inference

**How to treat it:** Never state as fact. Cite the source, note it's a single source, ask for a second confirmation. "One operator reports X (T3 — single source, unverified); I'd want to see this corroborated before building the thesis around it."

### T4 — Inferred
**What it is:** Conclusions the researcher draws from reasoning, not direct evidence.

Examples:
- "Your avatar probably has purchasing power because they run an agency"
- "The mechanism should work because similar approaches have worked in adjacent markets"
- Reconstructed competitor positioning from their copy
- "Based on what you've described, the break point is likely conversion, not fulfillment"

**How to treat it:** Label every inference explicitly. "This is inferred — here's how to confirm it."

---

## The most common T3 traps in offer research

**1. "The market is underserved"**
Almost always a founder's belief, not a market finding. Evidence required:
- Buyers actively searching and not finding (keyword data, forum posts, inbound DMs)
- Competitors with waitlists or backlogs
- The user is already getting unprompted inquiries

Without one of these, it's T3.

**2. Guru revenue figures**
"Alex Hormozi made $100M" — cannot be independently verified; may be lifetime, may be gross, may include equity. Treat all guru financial claims as T3. The *frameworks* they teach may be T1 (verifiable with your own tests); the *revenue claims* are not.

**3. "Our results are [X]"**
Any testimonial result without: the client's starting point, the timeline, the inputs they provided, and the base rate (how many others tried and failed) is T3. A single striking testimonial is a marketing asset; it is not a performance claim.

**4. Market size statistics**
"The market is worth $X" almost always comes from a paid research report with a vested interest in appearing large. Treat all TAM/SAM/SOM figures as T3 unless the methodology is disclosed and the source is independent.

---

## The one source that is always T1

**The user's own data on their best vs. worst clients.**

This is primary data. It is the most reliable offer signal available. If the user has closed five deals:
- What did the best two have in common?
- What did the worst two have in common?
- Who closed fastest and at the highest price?

This split is the compass. Everything else in offer research is helping the user understand whether the direction the compass points is scalable and buildable. Weight it heavily.
