# cost-curve-design.md — Scalability Architecture

## The four cost-curve archetypes

Every offer has a cost curve — the shape of how costs behave as volume grows. Most founders discover theirs accidentally, long after the offer is built.

| Archetype | Behavior | Example | Strategic position |
|-----------|----------|---------|-------------------|
| **Low fixed / high variable** | Cheap to start; every new client brings near-proportional cost | Full-service agency, generalist coaching | Cannot improve margins by growing; revenue grows but so does headcount |
| **High fixed / low variable** | Expensive to build; nearly free to serve each additional customer | Software, online courses, productized content | Every new client after breakeven is nearly pure margin |
| **Low / low** | Cheap to build AND cheap to serve | Lightweight info products, simple productized services | Rare and valuable; hard to protect |
| **High / high** | Expensive to build AND expensive to serve | Bespoke enterprise consulting, custom manufacturing | Only viable if pricing is extreme |

**The strategic instruction:** Design the offer so the cost curve flattens as you scale. Migrate from low-fixed/high-variable toward high-fixed/low-variable through productization and standardization.

## How the 3S formula changes the cost curve

When one specific avatar arrives with one specific problem to be solved one specific way:
- Fulfillment can be templated
- Onboarding can be standardized
- Execution can be delegated to less expensive team members following a documented process
- Parts of delivery can be automated (AI-augmented or fully automated)

The generalist cannot do this — every engagement is bespoke, variable cost stays high forever.

## Signals the user is on the wrong cost curve

- "Every client is different" / "we customize for each client" → variable cost is high and will stay high
- Adding clients requires adding proportional headcount → the offer is not productized
- Working 60+ hours per week at $30K–$100K/month with no margin to hire → no man's land trap
- Fulfillment is done personally by the founder → the offer cannot scale past the founder's time

## The done-for-you hybrid (cost curve strategy)

Own the one critical piece done-for-you (highest value, most feared by the client); coach or systematize the rest.

This holds premium pricing (the DFY piece) while protecting margin (not doing everything).

## LTV:CAC benchmark

Healthy: 3:1 minimum (customer lifetime value is at least 3x the cost to acquire them)
Under 1:1: Unsustainable; you're paying more to acquire clients than they're worth
Above 5:1: Often signals under-investment in acquisition — there may be more growth available

LTV:CAC is an *offer output*, not a marketing output:
- Specificity raises LTV (better-fit clients, higher price, longer retention)
- Cost curve determines the margin available to fund CAC
- Mechanism + proof determine conversion (which affects effective CAC)
